SPECIAL REPORT · COVER INVESTIGATION

Where Business Meets Government

An evidence-led account of private business, government power, clemency, contractors and oversight across the Trump administrations.

The Washington Record research compilation · October 31, 2026
Methodology: This is a historical compilation of existing documentary research. It contains attributed allegations and disputed claims, not 230 criminal findings. Read our standards.

Prologue — Following the documents

A hotel bill can become a constitutional question. A real-estate lease can raise questions about foreign influence. A pardon is unquestionably within presidential authority, yet its timing, beneficiaries and surrounding payments can still warrant examination. And a federal contract can fail spectacularly without proving that anyone took a bribe. Those differences are the starting point for this account.

The documentary trail crosses two Donald Trump administrations, the years between them, and businesses linked to relatives, appointees, donors, investors and contractors. It includes an official ruling on withheld Ukraine aid, a 2022 corporate criminal conviction, a Saudi-backed investment made after government service, a new wave of cryptocurrency ventures, a series of high-profile clemency decisions, and disputes over access to government records. The episodes are linked not by a single proven conspiracy but by a common institutional question: how does the public establish whether official power and private benefit remained separate?

The archive underlying this story contains 230 research entries, not 230 proven crimes. Some record official findings; others summarize attributed news investigations; several outline disputes in which material records remain unavailable. Forty-three are expressly classified as research leads. To inspect any claim, follow its case reference, read the qualification, and open its original cited source. The evidence standard should not change with the prominence of a name or the intensity of an allegation.

Chapter 1 — The businesses that did not disappear

When Trump entered office in 2017, businesses bearing his name continued to operate. That created an unusually concrete way to study the intersection of the presidency and private commerce: a customer could pay a Trump property while simultaneously doing business with the United States government. The public record shows that foreign-government spending at Trump-associated businesses became the subject of congressional investigation and constitutional litigation. It does not establish that every payment bought a policy decision.

House Oversight Committee Democratic staff documented at least $7.8 million from 20 foreign governments to four Trump businesses in the accounting records they reviewed. Those records covered only a subset of the business empire. A separate review by Citizens for Responsibility and Ethics in Washington estimated $13.6 million using a broader evidence base. These are competing scopes, not figures to add together. The congressional analysis raised emoluments questions, but the applicable litigation did not conclude with a trial verdict establishing that every documented transaction was illegal. C007 C008 C009 C010

Another paper trail concerns public money. Secret Service personnel used Trump-linked properties while providing protection, producing lodging bills and questions about pricing, necessity and appropriate separation from presidential financial interests. Political committees and campaign organizations also spent money at Trump properties. Those are measurable expenditures. Whether an individual payment crossed a legal line requires records about the transaction and applicable law, not a blanket conclusion drawn from its destination. C011 C076

The distinction matters throughout the dossier: knowing where money went is often easier than knowing why an official decision was made. Reporting cannot bridge that gap by treating temporal proximity as proof of a bargain. It can, however, identify precisely which contracts, communications, ethics waivers or beneficiary records are missing.

Chapter 2 — What courts and inspectors general actually found

Some episodes rest on more than suspicion. New York authorities obtained a $2 million court-ordered payment concerning misuse of charitable assets by the Trump Foundation. The Trump Organization's two corporate entities were convicted of criminal tax offenses and fined $1.61 million; Trump himself was not a charged defendant in that corporate tax prosecution. In 2024, a New York jury convicted Trump on 34 felony business-records counts; the January 2025 sentence was an unconditional discharge, without incarceration, probation or a fine. These separate outcomes must not be blended into one legal allegation. C002 C004 C006

The New York civil-fraud case requires particular care. An intermediate appellate court in August 2025 vacated a large monetary award while otherwise affirming portions of the civil judgment. A reader encountering the earlier dollar amount without its later appellate disposition could walk away with a materially false understanding. Its procedural history remains distinct from the criminal tax verdict and the 2024 business-records conviction. C001

The formal oversight record extends well beyond Trump's own companies. A federal inspector general found that 20 of 21 trips reviewed for former Health and Human Services Secretary Tom Price failed applicable travel requirements, with at least $341,000 in federal funds described as wasted. The Office of Government Ethics detailed omissions and inaccurate statements involving Commerce Secretary Wilbur Ross's financial disclosures, while distinguishing those ethics concerns from proof of a criminal conflict-of-interest violation. These are different types of findings, but both show why inspector-general reports and ethics correspondence are important starting points. C024 C023

For other officials, the record is more contested. The investigation of Elaine Chao's ties to family shipping interests and ethics reviews of Interior Secretary Ryan Zinke require attribution to the offices that conducted them, along with their documented limits. Being investigated, retaining a business asset, or having a family member with a commercial interest does not by itself establish criminal wrongdoing. C022 C097

Chapter 3 — Family networks and international capital

A major question in the period between Trump's terms is what former public officials did after leaving office. Jared Kushner, the former senior White House adviser and presidential son-in-law, launched Affinity Partners. Congressional records describe a $2 billion commitment from Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund despite objections recorded by investment staff. The amount was investment capital committed to a firm, not a $2 billion personal cash payment to Kushner. The records raise questions about post-government relationships and fund economics; they do not, by themselves, prove bribery. C028

Former Treasury Secretary Steven Mnuchin separately obtained Gulf-linked investment capital for Liberty Strategic Capital. Those transactions should be studied alongside Kushner's investment, but never combined as if they were payments to a shared beneficiary. Understanding management fees, ownership interests, regulatory decisions and any official communications is more informative than adding headline dollar figures. C029

A much-discussed transaction from the first term concerns Kushner Companies' 666 Fifth Avenue property. Brookfield entered a 99-year lease in 2018 for a reportedly approximately $1.28 billion prepaid arrangement. Brookfield had Qatar-linked investors elsewhere in its business, yet said the particular transaction was financed by a different fund without that participation. The public dossier therefore does not establish direct Qatari funding of this lease. That qualification is central, not a footnote. C012

There were other international points of contact: trademarks sought by Ivanka Trump-linked businesses in China; foreign real-estate development announcements after the 2024 election; and business relationships involving partners in the Gulf and Asia. Government approval of a trademark or a development permit is a fact to record. Showing an improper exchange for U.S. policy requires much more: the decision-maker, the timeline, the benefit, the policy act, and persuasive evidence connecting them. C013 C042 C043 C044 C045

Chapter 4 — Cryptocurrency creates new routes to money and access

A second-term investigation cannot be understood using hotel rooms and real estate alone. The Trump family's participation in digital-asset ventures created new revenue channels, investor relationships and regulatory questions. World Liberty Financial filed a federal securities offering notice in October 2024 describing a proposed offering far larger than the amount actually sold as of that filing. Treating the offering ceiling as revenue would produce a fundamental accounting error. Subsequent filings and financial disclosures require the same careful separation between gross proceeds, related-party payments and personal income. C030 C031 C032

A token-holder dinner offered access to the president to prominent participants. Reporting also connected investments in World Liberty with regulatory developments affecting some crypto-industry figures. Such arrangements raise questions about the sale of access and perceived conflicts; those questions cannot be answered by assuming every token purchase was a personal payment to Trump or that every agency action resulted from a transaction. C033 C034

One of the largest figures in the dossier is $2 billion, describing an Abu Dhabi-backed investment in Binance that World Liberty said would be settled using its USD1 stablecoin. The investment was into Binance, not a $2 billion purchase of World Liberty or an equal payment to the Trump family. Determining any economic gain to stablecoin affiliates requires separate information about reserve assets, fees and ownership. Another reported $500 million foreign-linked stake arrangement concerns a different transaction and should not be merged into the first. C035 C036

The government also shifted digital-asset enforcement policy and addressed corporate cases and pardons involving people in the industry. An April 2025 Justice Department memorandum set out a new enforcement approach. A policy change can benefit private companies without itself proving corrupt favoritism. A defensible account looks for concrete conflicts, departures from stated agency criteria, and communications between those seeking relief and the decision-makers. C037 C038 C040

Chapter 5 — Clemency, intermediaries and the limits of inference

Few presidential powers invite a sharper distinction between lawful authority and potential improper influence than clemency. A pardon may forgive federal offenses; a commutation changes a sentence without necessarily removing a conviction. The Constitution gives presidents broad discretion. That authority does not remove legitimate questions when lobbyists, donors, relatives and other intermediaries appear around a particular decision. The case archive separately indexes those episodes rather than implying that clemency itself is wrongdoing. C025 C064 C176 C177

One prominent second-term case involves Henry and Imelda Cuellar, pardoned in December 2025 while facing federal charges arising from alleged payments linked to foreign interests. Both denied wrongdoing, and there was no criminal trial verdict establishing their guilt. The administration's public treatment of the pardon and later political commentary warrant examination, but neither a pardon nor political rhetoric establishes a corrupt exchange. C134

A more complicated financial network surrounds the pardon of banker Julio Herrera Velutini. Reporting described a proposed employment arrangement involving Trump lawyer Alina Habba, political giving by the banker's daughter and lobbying by Ballard Partners. An official lobbying disclosure reported $630,000 for fourth-quarter 2025 banking-regulatory lobbying. A reported employment package exceeding $50 million is a proposed contract value, not verified cash paid. Habba and the White House denied she arranged the pardon. Those are facts and contested accounts that belong together in the same case file. C130 C131 C132 C133

The expanded archive includes clemency for people convicted of financial crimes, political corruption and drug-related offenses, including Ross Ulbricht, whose sentence was ended by a pardon, and Garnett Gilbert Smith and Larry Hoover, whose federal sentences were commuted. The distinction between a pardon and a commutation is legally significant, as is the absence of verified proof that any one of these particular drug-related decisions was purchased. Other entries document reported lobbying fees, donor relationships or questions about victim restitution on a case-by-case basis. C135 C136 C137 C145 C148 C178 C179 C180

What a credible review can say is that the clemency system creates opportunities for advocacy and access that are difficult for outsiders to audit. It cannot responsibly transform a donor's proximity or a paid lobbyist's activity into proof of an illegal bargain without the missing link. The online archive therefore gives every clemency case its own sources, legal action and caveat.

Chapter 6 — The officials who held power, including those temporarily appointed

Power can be exercised by an official whose title includes acting, interim or special. In the Justice Department, the line between lawful appointment and unauthorized exercise of prosecutorial authority became the subject of litigation. The dossier includes court decisions and disputes involving Alina Habba, Lindsey Halligan, Sigal Chattah, John Sarcone and Bilal Essayli. Those rulings concern legal authority to occupy or exercise particular offices. They are not automatic findings that the individuals committed bribery or fraud. C183 C184 C185 C186 C187 C188

Todd Blanche presents a different set of institutional questions. After serving as Trump's personal defense lawyer, he entered the Justice Department, served as acting attorney general and became the Senate-confirmed attorney general in August 2026. The transition makes recusals, handling of investigations and departmental independence subjects for record-based oversight. His former representation is not itself a violation. The relevant inquiry is whether the governing ethics rules were observed in particular matters. C181 C182

The same principle extends to cabinet officials, deputy secretaries, political advisers, regulators and people who hold financial interests through relatives or partnerships. The archive follows potentially relevant financial disclosures, stock transactions, industry affiliations, independent oversight access and ethics-waiver questions. It does not treat a former employer or a spouse's business as proof of misconduct. C056 C057 C063 C168 C170 C214 C216

An honest roster also has to acknowledge its limits. The dossier's officials-and-contractors table is a targeted coverage list, not a roster of every person who served in either administration. Financial-disclosure databases can substantially broaden a review, but they do not supply wrongdoing findings merely by listing an appointee. The companion site preserves the currently screened cases and unresolved avenues instead of pretending that the universe of personnel has been fully investigated. C169

Chapter 7 — Contractors, public works and who bears the cost

Federal contracting can leave the public with an unusually tangible result: a finished project, a bill and, sometimes, evidence that the work did not last. In 2026 the Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool became one such case. Interior issued no-bid contracts connected with a renovation and water-system work. An inspection described widespread failures of the newly installed coating, including at least 85 locations with deficiencies. Contractors, subcontractors, monitoring personnel and officials each appear in the record; those roles cannot be collapsed into a single allegation of intentional wrongdoing. C192 C193 C194

The dossier names Atlantic Industrial Coatings, Greenwater Services, Mid-America Industrial Coatings and other firms with distinct work scopes. An investigation cited improper materials and a rushed schedule. The administration and some participants disputed how much damage was caused by vandalism. Federal prosecutors subsequently described serious disclosure problems in cases based on vandalism allegations. These events warrant review of procurement exceptions, inspection findings, change orders, payments and communications. They do not prove, without additional evidence, that the contracts were awarded in exchange for favors. C195 C196 C197 C198 C199 C200

Other contracting questions are less visible but potentially consequential. The report tracks government-linked financing for private ventures, firms connected with senior appointees, defense and drone procurement, and the complicated overlap between Elon Musk's government role and the companies he controls. These matters vary widely in documented evidence. A subsidy, loan, contract ceiling, procurement award or investment is not equivalent to an illicit payment. C049 C050 C051 C053 C054 C055

The question in a contracting investigation is not simply whether a politically connected person benefited. It is whether decisions followed the governing competition, ethics, disclosure, procurement and performance rules. That requires a contract-by-contract approach, including the defenses and explanations offered by the awarding agency and vendors.

Chapter 8 — Private financing of public priorities

Donors do not disappear from political life once an administration takes office. The second-term archive examines donations and pledges tied to White House construction, companies with federal business, fundraising organizations, media settlements and political causes. Several entries concern the proposed ballroom project and its roster of private supporters. Reports about pledged contributions are not proof that the same amount was collected in cash, and a donor's government contracts do not automatically mean a purchase of influence occurred. C070 C071 C072 C073 C074 C075

Media-company settlements and commercial projects add another layer. The record contains a Paramount/CBS settlement, other litigation payments, a planned presidential-library financing structure and a documentary licensing agreement associated with Melania Trump. They are distinct commercial, legal and political transactions. Their relevant terms, decision-makers and distribution of proceeds must be checked independently. C077 C078 C079 C080

The Justice Department's Anti-Weaponization Fund illustrates why later developments matter. The department announced a proposed $1.776 billion mechanism in May 2026, then rescinded the establishing order in August. The later order stated that no members were appointed, no claims process created, no funds transferred and no claims paid. Calling it a functioning multibillion-dollar payout program after that rescission would be inaccurate. C091

Chapter 9 — When auditors challenge government action

Some of the clearest legal findings in the record are about control of appropriated money rather than private enrichment. The Government Accountability Office concluded that withholding certain Ukraine security assistance during Trump's first term violated the Impoundment Control Act. During the second term, GAO identified statutory violations concerning designated federal programs, including the Institute of Museum and Library Services, FEMA funds and federal health-research grants. These are determinations about agency compliance with appropriation law; they are not automatic criminal convictions of named officials. C015 C208 C209 C210 C211

The dossier also contains significant cautionary counterexamples. GAO did not find an Impoundment Control Act violation in every pause it reviewed, and found certain acting FEMA leadership appointments lawful. A publication that omits these narrower findings would distort the record, even if its purpose were only to document failures. C212 C213

DOGE provides another example. GAO and other reviewers raised questions about financial-disclosure and ethics controls, access to agency systems and calculations used to advertise savings. A reported savings number is not the same as audited money saved. The archival entries distinguish documented deficiencies and accounting disagreements from allegations that any particular official stole data or committed a crime. C164 C165 C166

The oversight process itself became contested in disputes over inspector-general access, agency officials' compliance with courts, and record-retention duties. Each episode calls for its own order, response and appellate status rather than an omnibus verdict on everyone involved. C170 C206 C219

Chapter 10 — The Epstein files and the burden of proof

A late addition to this archive concerns the federal handling of records related to Jeffrey Epstein and Ghislaine Maxwell. Congress enacted the Epstein Files Transparency Act on November 19, 2025, calling for disclosure of covered records subject to statutory exceptions, including protections for victims. The Justice Department announced a release totaling nearly 3.5 million pages in January 2026 and described a larger body of potentially responsive material. A numerical difference between records reviewed and records published does not by itself establish destroyed evidence or illegal concealment. C220 C221

Court proceedings offer a more precise picture. In Phang v. Blanche, a federal district court issued a preliminary injunction in June 2026 requiring the government to disclose or explain specified redactions, address underlying interview notes, review certain foreign-language material and satisfy publication requirements. In September, the court accepted several redactions on victim-protection grounds while ordering further review of other items; it declined to hold officials in contempt at that stage. These are meaningful judicial findings, but not convictions for perjury, obstruction or trafficking. C222 C223 C229

A report by Democratic staff of the House Judiciary Committee in October 2026 presented serious allegations involving Trump, Paolo Zampolli and other people, drawing on interviews and federal records. Trump and the White House denied the allegations. The staff's account is not a bipartisan committee adjudication or a judicial finding of criminal conspiracy. Similarly, published records of Blanche's interview with Maxwell can be examined, and critics can question the interview's direction, without asserting that a court found witness tampering. C224 C225 C226

The July 2025 DOJ–FBI memorandum, an announced inspector-general audit and a later Federal Register report belong in that chronology as well. The report rejects unauthenticated claims from a user-supplied supplement about supposedly missing alphanumeric evidence caches and deliberately purged financial ledgers. Those assertions had no substantiated chain of custody in the provided document. C227 C228 C229 C230

Epilogue — What the record proves, and what remains open

The strongest findings in this investigation are specific. Courts entered judgments, agencies issued documented policy decisions, inspectors general identified deficiencies, GAO reached statutory conclusions, businesses recorded investments and official clemency grants were signed. Other matters rest on reported transactions, investigative questions or contested interpretations. The report's evidence categories are a way of describing support for narrow propositions, not a measure of anyone's guilt.

The archive also illustrates common mistakes that can make political investigations misleading: adding unrelated financial amounts, treating a proposed contract as money already received, presenting a rescinded program as active, describing a commutation as a pardon, ignoring a later appellate ruling, or upgrading a partisan report into a criminal verdict. Each can change the meaning of a story. The site's case entries preserve the caveats so that readers can reconstruct the underlying record for themselves.

No public-source review can prove it has discovered every improper private transaction. Bank records, beneficial ownership, attorney communications, agency deliberations and other important materials may be unavailable. The 43 unresolved leads in the archive are therefore research assignments, not accusations. Readers should be able to disagree about the political significance of what has been documented without being misled about what the evidence actually establishes.

Editorial standard: Where the source establishes an act, the story states the act. Where a named source alleges misconduct, the story attributes that allegation. Where the records do not establish an exchange, intent or criminal liability, the story does not invent one.
